## 1.1 Introduction - Basics of Semiconductor Manufacturing

A semiconductor is a material that has electrical conductivity between that of a conductor and an insulator. The conductivity of a semiconductor can be controlled and modified by varying factors such as temperature, impurities, or an applied voltage. Increasing temperature leads to broken bonds and free electrons are generated. This ability to control conductivity makes semiconductors essential components in electronic devices.

The most commonly used semiconductor material is silicon (Si), although other materials like germanium (Ge) and gallium arsenide (GaAs) are also used. Silicon is particularly popular due to its abundance, stability, and excellent semiconductor properties.

Semiconductors, exemplified by materials like silicon, play a pivotal role in electronic devices as they act as switches, showcasing conductivity levels between conductors and insulators. Basic semiconductor components, such as diodes, function as on/off switches, allowing current to flow in one direction. More intricate transistors, like Metal-OxideSemiconductor Field-Effect Transistors (MOSFETs), operate by controlling the flow of current between the source and drain terminals via a voltage applied to the gate terminal. In the case of MOSFETs, the creation of an electric field by the gate terminal either permits or impedes the movement of charge carriers, serving as a switch. This switching capability is fundamental in digital circuits, where combinations of on/off states in transistors represent binary digits, forming the basis for logic gates, flip-flops, and digital information processing. The binary system, reliant on the ability of semiconductors to transition between states, is integral to encoding and manipulating data in electronic devices.

## 1.1.1 Moore's Law:

Moore's Law, an observation made by Gordon Moore, co-founder of Intel, in 1965, asserts that the number of transistors on a microchip doubles approximately every two years while the cost of computers is halved. This trend has driven exponential growth in computing power and significantly advanced technology over the past few decades. Fundamentally, while Moore's Law continues to inspire advancements in semiconductor technology by predicting a doubling of transistors every two years, the industry now faces significant challenges in maintaining this pace due to physical limits such as quantum tunneling, rising manufacturing costs, and diminishing performance gains. The semiconductor sector is adapting by adopting alternative strategies like 3D integration, heterogeneous integration, specialized chip architectures, and exploring new materials such as graphene. Although traditional transistor scaling is slowing, innovation persists through system-level optimizations and emerging technologies like quantum computing, ensuring continued progress in semiconductor performance (McKenzie, J., 2023, June 20).

## Phase 6: EOL (End of Life)

PPOR (Product Phase Out Request):

This is the initial trigger for the EOL process. A Product Phase Out Request (PPOR) is submitted when there is a need to discontinue a specific semiconductor product. This request can originate from various departments, such as product management, operations, or sales, typically due to reasons like declining demand, technological obsolescence, or the availability of newer alternatives.

EOL Decision:

Upon receiving the PPOR, the relevant stakeholders within the company, such as product management, operations, and possibly executive management, review the request. They assess factors like current demand, inventory levels, customer commitments, and potential impacts on the business. If the decision is made to proceed with phasing out the product, the EOL status is confirmed.

EOL:

Once the decision is made, the product officially enters the End-of-Life phase. This means that the company will stop manufacturing the product and start planning for its withdrawal from the market.

EOL Announcement:

An official EOL announcement is made to inform all relevant parties, including customers, distributors, and internal teams. This announcement typically includes key details like the final order date, last production date, and any recommended replacement products.

ECN/PCN out Production Status BOT:

ECN (Engineering Change Notice) or PCN (Product Change Notification) refers to official notices that are sent to customers about changes in product status, including EOL. This part of the flow likely refers to an automated BOT that handles the dissemination of these notices and updates the production status accordingly.

## Step1: Silicon Wafer Manufacturing

The majority of semiconductor wafers are made from silicon, which is extracted from sand, a material abundant in silicon dioxide (SiO2). The production process begins by heating the sand until it melts and undergoes purification to produce high-purity liquid silicon. This molten silicon is then allowed to solidify through a process called crystallization, resulting in the formation of a silicon rod, commonly known as an ingot.

The ingot is then sliced into thin, disc-shaped pieces known as wafers. However, after slicing, the surface of these wafers tends to be rough and may contain various defects that could impact their quality and performance. To ensure a smooth, defect-free surface, polishing machines are used to refine the wafers, preparing them for subsequent processing steps in semiconductor manufacturing.

## Step2: Oxidation - Create Oxide Film on Wafer Surface

The silicon wafer produced in the initial step of semiconductor manufacturing is not inherently conductive and requires further processing to achieve its semiconductive properties. This is accomplished through an oxidation process. During oxidation, oxygen or water vapor is introduced to the wafer surface, forming a uniform oxide film.

This oxide film plays a crucial role in protecting the wafer's surface. It serves as an insulating layer that prevents current leakage between different circuit elements on the wafer, thus ensuring proper electrical isolation and functionality of the integrated circuits. The formation of this oxide layer is a fundamental step in creating a semiconductive environment necessary for the fabrication of complex semiconductor devices.

## Step3: Photolithography - Draw Circuit Design on Wafer

Sends the completed design (GDS) to the foundry for wafer fabrication, marking the transition from design to manufacturing.

A photomask acting as a film that transfers circuit designs onto wafers. It is a glass substrate that contains a computer-designed pattern, representing the intricate layout of the electronic circuits. To imprint this pattern onto a wafer, a photoresist-a light-sensitive material-is applied to the wafer's surface.

Once the photoresist is in place, light is passed through the patterned photomask, and the circuit design is transferred onto the wafer. The areas of the photoresist exposed to the light undergo chemical changes, allowing the circuit pattern to be etched or developed onto the wafer's surface during subsequent processing steps. This process, known as photolithography, is fundamental for fabricating integrated circuits.

## Patterning Process

The pattern formation process in semiconductor manufacturing involves transferring mask patterns onto a wafer, followed by etching to create the desired structures. Photolithography is used to print these patterns and includes several key steps:

In the semiconductor manufacturing process, photolithography is a critical step for patterning the wafer's surface. This process involves several stages, beginning with the resist coat, where a photosensitive material known as photoresist is uniformly applied to the wafer. The wafer is spun at high speeds to achieve the desired thickness of the resist layer, ensuring uniformity.

Following this, the wafer undergoes a preexposure bake (soft bake), which increases the adhesion of the photoresist and removes any remaining solvents. This step prepares the wafer for the upcoming pattern transfer. Next, mask alignment is performed, where the photomask-containing the intricate circuit pattern to be transferred-is carefully aligned with the wafer. Precise alignment marks are used to ensure the accuracy of this positioning.

In the exposure phase, the wafer is subjected to ultraviolet (UV) light, which passes through the photomask to selectively expose the photoresist layer. The exposure causes chemical changes in the photoresist, allowing the desired pattern to be transferred onto the wafer. After exposure, the wafer undergoes a postexposure bake (PEB), which further enhances the properties of the photoresist, such as its solubility, and reduces potential defects like standing waves.

The next step is developing, where a developer solution is applied to the wafer to remove either the exposed or unexposed regions of the photoresist, depending on the type of resist being used (positive or negative). This step reveals the patterned material on the wafer. To increase the stability of the remaining photoresist, a hard bake is performed. This hightemperature bake solidifies the photoresist, improving its thermal stability and adherence for subsequent processing steps.

After the pattern has been established, the resist strip process removes the remaining photoresist, leaving behind only the patterned material on the wafer. Finally, the material removal phase, using wet or dry etching, removes the unprotected material from the wafer. Wet etching utilizes liquid chemicals for isotropic (uniform in all directions) removal, while dry etching employs gases or ions for anisotropic (directional) removal, allowing for more precise patterning.

## Step4: Etching - Remove Unnecessary Materials

Remove unnecessary materials from the wafer surface so that only the design pattern remains. This is done using a wet or dry etching technique. The photolithography process and the etching process are repeated several times on the wafer layer by layer depending on how many layers are used.

## 1.3.3 SMT/Production:

The SMT/Production process begins with Parts in House and Boards in House, where electronic components and printed circuit boards (PCBs) are received from suppliers and thoroughly inspected to ensure they meet the required quality standards. Parts are checked for specifications and stored properly, while PCBs are examined for defects like warping or incorrect dimensions. Once these materials are approved, they move to the PCB Assembly stage. Here, solder paste is applied to the PCB, and automated pick-and-place machines position the components onto the board. The assembly process is completed with reflow soldering, securing the components, and followed by Automated Optical Inspection (AOI) to identify any assembly defects.

After assembly, the boards undergo Evaluation Testing to ensure they meet functionality and performance specifications. This includes electrical testing, in-circuit testing, and functional verification, all crucial for identifying and addressing potential issues before mass production. Once the product passes all evaluation tests, it is Released to Production, marking the transition to large-scale manufacturing. This stage focuses on optimizing the production process through continuous monitoring and improvement efforts such as DPPM (Defects Per Million Parts) Measurement and Improvement to reduce defects, Yield Improvement to increase the percentage of good units, and Product Sustaining to maintain and enhance product quality over time.

To ensure the ongoing efficiency and effectiveness of the production process, Monthly Manufacturing Reviews are conducted. These reviews assess production efficiency, quality control, and yield rates, allowing for adjustments and improvements as needed. Finally, as the product nears the end of its lifecycle, an End of Life (EOL) Decision and Announcement is made. This process involves evaluating remaining inventory, planning the phase-out of production, and ensuring customers are informed and supported during the transition. The EOL announcement marks the official discontinuation of the product, with strategies in place to manage the end-of-life phase smoothly and efficiently. At the end, Production involves scaling up the manufacturing process to meet the required volume and demand for the product. This stage includes packaging the finished products, coordinating logistics, and ensuring efficient distribution.

## 1.4 Fabless vs IDM

In the semiconductor industry, the terms "Fabless" and "Integrated Device Manufacturer (IDM)" represent two distinct business models that companies adopt to manage the design and production of semiconductor products.

Fabless (Fabrication-Less) semiconductor companies focus solely on the design, research, development, and marketing of semiconductor products without owning or operating their own manufacturing facilities. Instead, they rely on third-party foundries to fabricate their designs. By outsourcing the fabrication process, fabless companies can concentrate their resources on innovation and design while leveraging the specialized

## 2.2.6 Additional Variations

Process variations in semiconductor manufacturing are influenced by several critical factors, each of which can have a significant impact on device performance and reliability. One key factor is gate dielectric variation, where inconsistencies in the thickness and properties of the gate dielectric layer affect gate control and leakage currents. Similarly, oxide thickness variations (Qox), or differences in the thickness of the oxide layer, can alter device performance by causing variations in threshold voltage and other key parameters.

Fixed charge variations in the oxide or other dielectric layers also contribute to process variability, as changes in the amount and distribution of fixed charge can influence the behavior of the device. Moreover, defects and traps present in the semiconductor or dielectric layers can affect carrier mobility and shift the threshold voltage, further impacting performance.

The Chemical Mechanical Planarization (CMP) process, used to smooth wafer surfaces, can introduce variability in wafer planarity. These variations in planarity can influence subsequent processing steps, leading to deviations in the final device characteristics. Shallow Trench Isolation (STI) step variations, responsible for electrically isolating different device regions, can also affect leakage currents and overall device performance.

Another contributing factor is metal gate height variability, where inconsistencies in gate height can influence electrical characteristics and threshold voltage. Additionally, inter-layer dielectric (ILD) and interconnect thickness variations can affect the electrical performance and reliability of the device, particularly in terms of signal integrity and insulation properties.

Lastly, strain variations in the semiconductor material, which are introduced to improve carrier mobility, can fluctuate and result in uneven device performance. These

## 3. Qualifications

Qualification testing is a critical phase in the integrated circuit (IC) manufacturing process, serving to ensure that the produced devices meet the stringent standards and performance criteria required before mass production or commercial release. The primary objective of these tests is to assess the ICs' adherence to design specifications and quality benchmarks, thereby identifying any potential manufacturing defects or deviations that could impair performance.

A key component of qualification testing is the assessment of reliability, which involves subjecting ICs to a range of environmental conditions, such as extreme temperatures and voltage variations, as well as other operational stresses. These tests evaluate the devices' durability and reliability across various scenarios. Functional testing is also performed to confirm that the IC operates as intended by verifying the functionality of its internal components and ensuring it meets the specified electrical and logical requirements.

The performance evaluation phase thoroughly examines critical parameters, such as speed, power consumption, and signal integrity, ensuring that the IC meets its desired performance targets. To further assess long-term reliability, endurance testing is conducted, where ICs are subjected to prolonged operational conditions, identifying any degradation in performance over time and ensuring the devices' ability to withstand extended use.

Another important aspect of qualification testing is ensuring compliance with industry and regulatory standards, which is vital for meeting customer requirements and ensuring the ICs can be effectively integrated into various electronic systems. In instances where a device fails during testing, a detailed failure analysis is conducted to determine the root cause. The insights gained from this analysis are instrumental in refining the manufacturing process and preventing similar issues in future production runs.

## 3.2. CDM vs HBM

CDM and HBM are two different ESD testing methods that simulate distinct ESD scenarios. CDM focuses on rapid discharge from a charged device, often used for semiconductor testing, while HBM simulates the discharge from a person's body to assess the ESD robustness of devices under typical usage conditions. Manufacturers may use both CDM and HBM testing to ensure the overall ESD resilience of their products.

## 1. ESD Scenario Simulated:

CDM: CDM simulates the discharge of accumulated charge on an electronic device that can occur during normal handling, transportation, or contact with other materials. It typically represents rapid discharge from a charged device to a grounded object, like when a device meets a charged metal tool.

HBM: HBM simulates the discharge of static electricity that can build up on a person's body and then be transferred to an electronic device when touched. It represents the ESD event that happens when a person touches a device after building up static charge, such as walking across a carpeted floor.

## 2. Test Circuit Configuration:

CDM: CDM testing typically involves a discharge from a charged pin or connector on the device to a grounded reference plane or structure. The device itself is charged and then rapidly discharged.

HBM: HBM testing involves a discharge from a human model, which is usually a specified resistor and capacitor network, to the device under test. The human model is represented as a resistor in series with a capacitor, and the discharge is initiated through direct contact with the device.

## 3. Voltage Levels:

CDM: CDM testing typically uses higher voltage levels compared to HBM testing. The voltage levels in CDM testing are usually in the range of 250V to 2,000V.

HBM: HBM testing typically uses lower voltage levels. The standard HBM test level is up to 2,000V.

## 4. Application and Suitability:

CDM: CDM testing is particularly relevant for assessing the ESD robustness of semiconductor devices and integrated circuits (ICs) because it simulates a charge that can accumulate on the device during manufacturing, handling, or packaging.

HBM: HBM testing is more relevant to assess the susceptibility of electronic devices to ESD events that can occur when a person touches the device. It is used to ensure that devices are robust enough to withstand ESD events during normal usage and handling by end-users.

The first step involves defining the acceptance criteria, which are grounded in industry standards, customer requirements, and specific quality specifications. These criteria delineate the acceptable quality levels for the product or component being tested.

Once the acceptance criteria are established, the lot size (N), representing the total number of units in the production lot, is determined. Subsequently, a suitable sampling plan is selected. This plan specifies the sample size (n), as well as the Acceptance Quality Level (AQL) and Rejection Quality Level (RQL), and is often based on widely recognized standards such as ANSI/ASQ Z1.4, military standards, or tailored customer requirements.

Using the chosen sampling plan and the determined lot size (N), the sample size (n) is calculated, representing the number of items that will be randomly selected from the lot for testing. Random sampling is critical to ensure that the selected items accurately represent the quality of the entire lot.

The selected sample is then subjected to a series of inspections and tests, in line with the predefined acceptance criteria. These tests may include visual inspections, functional assessments, measurements, and other relevant evaluations. After testing, the number of defective items in the sample is counted, and the LAT result is calculated using the formula:

## LAT (%) = (Number of Defective Items / Sample Size) x 100.

The LAT result is then compared with the established AQL and RQL thresholds. If the LAT result is less than or equal to the AQL, the lot is accepted. Conversely, if the LAT result exceeds the RQL, the lot is rejected. In cases where the LAT result falls between the AQL and RQL, further action may be necessary to decide the lot's fate.

Thorough documentation of the LAT process is essential. This includes recording the sampling plan, sample selection, test results, and the decision to either accept or reject the lot. Such documentation ensures traceability and forms part of the quality control records. If a lot is rejected, corrective actions must be initiated to address the identified quality issues. These actions may include rework, sorting, investigation, or implementing process improvements. Finally, insights gained from the LAT process are valuable for continuous improvement efforts, helping to enhance manufacturing processes and product design to prevent future quality issues.

WAT (Wafer Acceptance Test): WAT is performed on each individual wafer before it undergoes the manufacturing process to become a finished semiconductor device. LAT (Lot Acceptance Test): LAT occurs after the semiconductor devices have been manufactured, packaged, and assembled into lots. It is the final test to determine whether the entire lot of devices meets the specified criteria before they are shipped to customers.

WAT (Wafer Acceptance Test): WAT occurs much earlier in the semiconductor manufacturing process, typically at the wafer fabrication stage. It is performed on each individual wafer as part of the quality control process to identify defects and ensure the wafers meet the required specifications.

## 3. Purpose:

LAT (Lot Acceptance Test): The primary purpose of LAT is to assess the overall quality and performance of a lot of finished semiconductor devices. It helps ensure that the entire batch of devices meets the specified criteria and is ready for shipment to customers.

WAT (Wafer Acceptance Test): The main purpose of WAT is to identify defects and assess the quality of individual wafers before they undergo further processing. It helps in optimizing the semiconductor manufacturing process by identifying and addressing issues at the wafer level, ultimately improving yield and quality.

## 4.2. Automated Test Equipment [ATE]

Automated test equipment (ATE) for semiconductors is a type of test equipment that sends automatic test patterns to the semiconductor device under test (DUT) and observes the output signals produced by it. ATE is used by semiconductor manufacturers to screen for faulty products, measure performance, and verify functionality of semiconductor devices. ATE can also perform stress tests, burn-in tests, and environmental tests on semiconductor devices to ensure their reliability and durability.

Automated test equipment for semiconductors is usually performed at different stages of the semiconductor manufacturing process, such as wafer testing, package testing, and board testing.

⎯ Wafer level testing is conducted on integrated circuits (ICs) while they are still on the silicon wafer, before they are separated into individual dies and packaged. This testing uses automated test equipment (ATE) with probe cards to contact the tiny pads on the wafer's surface.

Key Characteristics:

Early Detection: Identifies defects early in the manufacturing process.

Cost-Effective: Saves costs by identifying non-functional dies before they undergo expensive packaging processes.

Yield Analysis: Provides insights into manufacturing yield and potential areas for improvement.

Controlled Environment: Conducted in a cleanroom environment with specialized equipment.

⎯ Package level testing occurs after the ICs have been separated from the wafer and assembled into their final packages. This stage ensures that the packaged ICs function correctly and meet specifications.

Key Characteristics:

Post-Packaging Verification: Confirms that the packaging process hasn't introduced any defects.

## Test Vectors and Automated Testing

In the realm of IC design and testing, test vectors are indispensable tools that enable engineers to verify the functionality and performance of integrated circuits. These test vectors consist of sequences of binary values applied to the IC's inputs, with the outputs observed and compared against expected results.

Test vectors are crucial for validating and verifying integrated circuit (IC) functionality during the semiconductor manufacturing process. They serve as a set of predefined inputs and expected outputs used to assess whether an IC operates correctly under various conditions. The key components of test vectors include input stimuli, which consist of binary values applied to the IC's input pins to test specific parts of the circuit. These input stimuli are paired with expected outputs, the anticipated binary values at the output pins corresponding to the inputs, used to verify correct operation. Control signals, which set the IC into specific modes or manage its internal states, and timing information, detailing the relationship between inputs and expected outputs, are also critical to ensure that the IC operates correctly at the intended clock speeds.

There are several types of test vectors, each serving a different purpose. Functional test vectors ensure that the IC performs its intended functions under a variety of conditions, while structural test vectors focus on the physical implementation of the IC and test fault models such as stuck-at, bridging, or delay faults. Parametric test vectors measure electrical characteristics such as voltage levels, currents, and timing to confirm that the IC meets its specified performance criteria.

Test vectors can be generated through different methods. Manual generation is suitable for simpler designs, where design engineers create test vectors based on their knowledge of the IC. For more complex designs, Automatic Test Pattern Generation (ATPG) tools are often used to create test vectors from the IC's netlist and fault models, providing high fault coverage. Simulation-based generation employs simulation tools to generate test vectors by simulating the IC design under a range of conditions.

Test vectors are typically applied during the manufacturing process through automated test equipment (ATE). In this setup, the test vectors are loaded into the ATE, which applies the input stimuli to the IC and captures the resulting outputs. These outputs are compared against the expected values to identify any discrepancies. If mismatches are found, the test engineer analyzes the failing vectors to determine the cause and resolve the issue, ensuring that the IC performs as intended.

The use of test vectors offers several benefits, including fault detection, which helps identify manufacturing defects and design flaws, and quality assurance, which confirms that the IC meets the required functional and performance criteria. Test vectors also contribute to yield improvement by diagnosing and resolving issues that could otherwise reduce the number of functional ICs produced. Finally, they help ensure reliability, as catching potential problems early in the production process enhances the long-term dependability of the ICs.

By implementing well-designed test vectors, manufacturing engineers can thoroughly validate IC designs, ensuring that only fully functional and reliable chips are shipped to the market.

## 5.1.2 Wire bond Verses Flip-chip

In semiconductor packaging, wire bonding and flip-chip are two distinct methods used to connect integrated circuits to their substrates or packages. Each method has its own advantages and applications, reflecting differences in design, performance, and manufacturing processes.

<!-- review-required: visual-evidence -->

Wire Bonding is a traditional method of semiconductor packaging where fine wires are used to establish electrical connections between the die and the lead frame or substrate. This process begins with wafer-level testing to identify and sort the dies. Once sorted, individual dies are attached to a lead frame using a die attach process. Electrical connections are then made through wire bonding, where thin gold or aluminum wires are bonded from the die's bond pads to the lead

frame's corresponding leads. This method is characterized by its flexibility and suitability for a wide range of applications, including those with moderate performance requirements and larger package sizes. Wire bonding is often favored for its cost-effectiveness and ease of implementation, particularly in high-volume production.

Flip-Chip Bonding, in contrast, involves a more advanced approach where the die is flipped and bonded directly onto the substrate or package. This process begins with wafer sorting, followed by the application of solder bumps or other conductive materials on the die's contact pads. The die is then flipped and placed onto the substrate, and a reflow process melts the solder bumps, establishing electrical connections between the die and the substrate. After reflow, additional steps such as cleaning, underfilling, and curing enhance mechanical stability and thermal performance. Flip-

It is particularly advantageous for high-density interconnections and applications requiring superior electrical and thermal characteristics. The flip-chip method is also conducive to higher I/O counts and improved signal integrity due to the direct connection of the die to the substrate.

Wire bonding is well-suited for applications where moderate performance and larger package sizes are acceptable. It is commonly used in consumer electronics and various other industries where cost considerations are paramount. Its simplicity and adaptability make it a prevalent choice in many standard packaging scenarios.

## 5.1.3 Under Bump Metallization Verses Pads

Under Bump Metallization (UBM) and pads are two critical elements used in the interconnection of integrated circuits with substrates, particularly in flip-chip and wire bonding technologies. These elements serve distinct roles in ensuring the structural and electrical integrity of the connections between the semiconductor die and its packaging or substrate.

<!-- page: 99 -->

Pads are the conductive areas on the semiconductor die that serve as the points of contact for electrical connections to the external circuit. In traditional wire bonding, bonding pads are usually made of aluminum or gold and are placed on the die's surface. These pads provide the necessary surface area for attaching the fine wires that connect the die to the lead frame or substrate. The size and positioning of the pads are crucial for optimizing wire bond reliability and performance. Pads are typically larger in size in wire-bonding applications to accommodate the wire bond loop height and mechanical strength required to secure the connection. In these setups, the

pads must be mechanically strong and electrically conductive to ensure optimal performance of the wire bonds over the life of the semiconductor device.

In contrast, Under Bump Metallization (UBM) is a specialized layer of metallization applied between the bonding pads and the solder bumps in flip-chip bonding technology. UBM consists of multiple metal layers that serve several purposes: it provides adhesion between the solder bumps and the silicon die, protects the die from potential damage during bonding, and ensures electrical connectivity. The UBM typically comprises layers of metals such as chromium, copper, nickel, or titanium, each serving distinct roles in the bonding process. The base metal layer ensures strong adhesion to the silicon or other dielectric materials, while the top layers provide a barrier

to prevent solder diffusion and improve the wetting properties of the solder bumps. UBM also helps to distribute mechanical stress across the bump area, enhancing the overall reliability of the flip-chip package by reducing the likelihood of cracking or failure under thermal and mechanical loads.

## 5.1.4 Wire bonding Verses Bumps

In semiconductor packaging, bond wires and pads, as well as bumps and UBM, form closely related pairs within their respective interconnection methods, each serving distinct purposes that align with the overall approach and requirements of the technology used.

In wire bonding, bond wires are directly connected to the bonding pads on the die, creating a reliable electrical connection. The size and positioning of the pads are designed to accommodate the bond wires, ensuring that the connection is both mechanically strong and electrically efficient. The relationship between bond wires and pads is foundational in this method, where the wire provides the pathway for electrical signals while the pads serve as the attachment points.

In contrast, flip-chip bonding uses bumps and UBM in tandem to establish direct connections between the die and the substrate. The bumps rely on the UBM to ensure strong adhesion and electrical integrity, as the UBM acts as a metallurgical interface that enhances the durability and reliability of the connection. This relationship is more advanced compared to bond wires and pads, as the bumps and UBM allow for higher I/O densities and better thermal management.

## 5.3.3 Fan-Out Wafer-Level Packaging (FOWLP)

## C. Through-Silicon Vias (TSVs)

TSV represent a critical advancement in semiconductor packaging and 3D integration, providing a vertical electrical connection through a silicon wafer or die. These conductive pathways traverse the entire thickness of the silicon, enabling communication between various layers or dies in a stacked configuration. This technology is fundamental to achieving high-density integration and enhancing the overall performance of semiconductor devices.

The creation of TSVs involves the etching of small holes through the silicon wafer, which are subsequently filled with a conductive material, typically copper. These vias establish direct electrical connections between the stacked chips, facilitating efficient communication between the different components within the package. TSVs offer a significant improvement over traditional interconnection methods, such as wire bonding and flip-chip, by drastically reducing the length of the electrical interconnects. This reduction improves signal integrity, minimizes latency, and contributes to higher performance.

This effective heat dissipation enhances the reliability and longevity of the semiconductor device, making it suitable for high-performance applications.

## B. Intellectual Property (IP)

The IP cores are essential reusable design blocks that simplify the development of semiconductor devices. For fabless semiconductor companies, understanding the types of IP cores, their associated economic benefits, and the procedures for becoming a verified IP provider, including achieving the critical "silicon proven" status and obtaining certification from industry leaders like TSMC, is crucial to improving their market position and financial performance.

IP cores are primarily divided into two categories: Hard IP and Soft IP. Hard IP refers to predesigned and pre-verified blocks of intellectual property that are provided in a fixed physical layout format, typically optimized for specific silicon processes. Delivered as netlists or GDSII files, Hard IP offers the advantage of a stable, unmodifiable physical design, which allows for optimized performance and integration with a fixed layout. ## 5.4 Glossary

3D bonding: A process that joins two or more wafer or die surfaces together.

3D interconnection technology: Technologies that enable vertical stacking of electronic components connected by two-dimensional (2D) interconnect layers.

3D packaging: Refers to technologies where a significant portion of die-to-die connections are not in a planar configuration relative to the package substrate.

3D stacking: A 3D bonding process that also establishes electrical connections between different device layers.

3D System-in-Package (3D-SIP): Three-dimensional (3D) integration using established package technologies, such as wire bonding or package-on-package stacking.

3D wafer-level packaging (3D-WLP): Three-dimensional (3D) integration utilizing wafer-level package technologies, such as redistributing electrical interconnects.

Chemical mechanical polishing or planarization (CMP): A wafer cleaning method used for both metal and dielectric layers, typically to prepare the surface for the next layer of material.

Embedded passives: Passive components integrated into an IC, added on top of an IC by adding a layer, embedded in a built-up polymer interconnect layer, or embedded in the package substrate.

Flip-chip ball grid array (FC-BGA): Similar to a plastic ball grid array (PBGA), where the die is interconnected to the substrate through the flip-chip process, where the die faces downward with interconnections made via solder bumps on the die. The space between the die and substrate is usually filled with an underfill material.

Flip-chip land grid array (FC-LGA): Similar to FC-BGA but without solder balls on the array of contact lands on the substrate.

Integrated passives: Arrays or networks of passive components (resistors, capacitors, and inductors) integrated onto a single substrate to form a complete passive component.

Mean time between failures (MTBF): The average time between failures in repairable or redundant systems.

## 6.1 Failure & Analysis

## A. Introduction to Failure Analysis:

Failure analysis is a systematic approach used to investigate and determine the causes of product or component failures within a manufacturing setting. It is an essential process for improving product quality, reliability, and manufacturing efficiency. By understanding why a product or component fails, manufacturers can implement corrective actions, optimize processes, and prevent future failures.

The input to the failure analysis process is failed devices. In some cases, very specific devices such as qualification failures must be successfully analyzed. In other cases such as yield analysis, where it is not feasible to analyze every failed device, a selection process must occur. The selection is usually made in order to make the biggest possible improvement in the manufacturing process. This typically consists of a Pareto ranking of defect characteristics observed. The goal of the failure analysis process is to enable an improvement process. Hence, the results of the failure analysis, the identification of a physical cause of failure, must be useful for correcting a manufacturing problem. This drives a need for "root cause" identification.

## 6.2 Methodology of Failure Analysis:

## 6.2.1 Electrical Characterization

Electrical testing is a critical initial step in failure analysis, providing essential insights into the electrical behavior of a failing device. This process begins with a basic electrical verification, often involving data logs that capture continuity, parametric, and functional outputs from production test equipment. The outcomes of this initial verification guide subsequent analysis, possibly leading to more detailed electrical characterization.

<!-- page: 125 -->

<!-- review-required: visual-evidence -->

Visual evidence on page 125 requires source review; no verified description is available.

<!-- review-end -->

For instance, detecting electrical leakage may necessitate in-depth I-V (current-voltage) analysis using tools like a curve tracer or parametric analyzer. In cases of functional failures, techniques such as Schmoo plotting or scan testing might be employed to better understand the failure's characteristics. Advanced electrical testing can sometimes pinpoint the failure site with high precision, especially in structured devices like memory components, where it may identify a defect as small as a single memory bit. In logic circuits with Design for Test (DFT) features, failures can often be isolated to a specific circuit node or net.

Electrical testing also plays a crucial role in identifying package-related issues and determining the need for non-destructive package analysis. Furthermore, successful fail site isolation depends on two key requirements: placing the device in its failing electrical state, which is understood through initial electrical characterization, and ensuring access to the die for further analysis.

## 6.2.2 Die Exposure

Die exposure is essential for failure site isolation techniques due to the opaque nature of packaging materials. Direct access to the device is required, and this is typically achieved by mechanically opening Ceramic packages or using a decapsulation process, such as jet etch, for plastic-encapsulated devices. Maintaining the electrical integrity of the device during die exposure is crucial to ensure that it continues to fail electrically in the same manner as before. Additionally, the external pins and their connections to the die must remain intact to facilitate convenient biasing for failure site isolation.

In most cases, the active surface of the die is exposed. However, for certain technologies, such as flip-chip devices or some memory devices where bond wires obscure much of the die surface, it may not be feasible to expose the active face while maintaining electrical functionality. In such cases, alternatives include exposing and polishing the back surface of the die for backside failure site isolation techniques, or completely removing the die from the package. Removing the die necessitates alternative methods for electrical stimulation, which can be achieved by repackaging the die in a package better suited for failure analysis or using a probe card.

## 6.2.3 Fail Site Isolation

Fail Site Isolation involves a range of techniques aimed at narrowing the focus of diagnostic investigations in integrated circuits (ICs), which contain millions of transistors and interconnects. Given this complexity, it is essential to reduce the scope of failure analysis to a manageable number of elements.

## The techniques used for failing site isolation can be broadly classified into two categories:

Global Techniques: These methods identify secondary effects of failures, such as heat generation or anomalous light emission. Thermal detection techniques and Photon Emission Microscopy are examples that respectively detect heat and light produced at failure sites. Other techniques in this category identify various carrier-generated or thermal events, facilitating rapid fail site isolation.

Probe Techniques: These methods involve direct measurement of electrical signals within the IC to troubleshoot circuit performance. However, without prior isolation of the failure site through electrical testing, the process of probing can be prolonged and tedious.

## 6.2.4 Packaging Analysis

Package Analysis involves non-destructive techniques to examine the internal structures of an integrated circuit (IC) package. When electrical data suggests a package-related issue, such as opens, shorts, or leakages, assessing the physical condition of the package and its interior before conducting any destructive analysis is crucial. These techniques also help in identifying the physical causes of open and short failures. Tools like X-radiography and Scanning Acoustic Microscopy (SAM) are particularly effective in providing valuable insights into the structural integrity of the package.

## 6.2.5 Physical & Chemical Analysis

Physical and Chemical Analysis is essential for identifying the physical cause of failure after the failure site has been isolated. This analysis typically involves three types of tools: sample preparation, physical observation, and chemical analysis.

⎯ Sample Preparation: Techniques like deprocessing, parallel polishing, and crosssectioning are used to remove materials and access the failure site.

⎯ Physical Observation: This is conducted using various types of microscopes, including optical, scanning electron, and transmission electron microscopes.

⎯ Chemical Analysis: Performed on defects where it can provide critical insights for determining the "root cause" of the failure.

## 6.4 Common Failure Mechanisms in Manufacturing

Understanding common failure mechanisms is essential for conducting effective failure analysis. Some of the most prevalent failure mechanisms in manufacturing include:

Material Defects: Failures caused by inherent defects in the materials used, such as impurities, voids, or grain boundaries. Common examples include:

⎯ Intermetallic Formation: In solder joints, the formation of intermetallic compounds can lead to brittle fractures.

⎯ Corrosion: Environmental exposure to moisture, chemicals, or salts can cause corrosion, leading to material degradation and failure.

Manufacturing Process Errors: Defects introduced during the manufacturing process, such as improper soldering, misalignment, or inadequate curing. Examples include:

⎯ Delamination: Separation of layers in a composite material, often due to poor adhesion or thermal cycling.

⎯ Solder Joint Failure: Caused by improper soldering techniques, leading to weak or brittle joints.

Design Flaws: Design-related issues that lead to failures under normal operating conditions. Examples include:

⎯ Thermal Overstress: Designs that do not adequately dissipate heat, leading to overheating and thermal failure.

⎯ Mechanical Overstress: Designs that concentrate stress in certain areas, leading to fractures or deformation.

Environmental Factors: External factors such as temperature, humidity, or corrosion that can lead to product failure. Examples include:

⎯ Electromigration: The movement of metal atoms due to high current density, leading to void formation and open circuits.

⎯ Thermal Cycling: Repeated heating and cooling that causes materials to expand and contract, leading to fatigue and failure.

## 7.3.1 Application Specific Integrated Circuit [ASIC]

An Application-Specific Integrated Circuit (ASIC) is a custom-built chip designed for a specific job or product. Unlike general-purpose chips, which can be used in many devices, ASICs are made to do one task really well. This gives them better performance and power efficiency. However, developing an ASIC is expensive, often costing millions of dollars in design and setup. Because of this, ASICs are usually used in products that are made in large numbers, like smartphones, cars, and computers.

ASICs can replace several standard components with a single chip, reducing the number of parts needed in a device. For products made in large volumes, this can lead to significant cost savings, even though the initial development is expensive.

While ASICs are expensive to design upfront, they become much cheaper per unit when produced in large numbers. This makes them more affordable in the long run for highvolume products.

## 7.3.3 Types of ASIC:

Types of ASICs: Semi-Custom and Full-Custom Design

Application-Specific Integrated Circuits (ASICs) are specially designed chips created for a particular task or product. There are two main types: Semi-Custom and Full-Custom ASICs. The way they are designed, the amount of customization they offer, and their overall cost differ greatly. Understanding these differences helps in choosing the best option for each project.

Semi-Custom ASICs are built using a mix of pre-designed components (like logic gates and flip-flops) along with custom-made parts. This means that part of the chip can be tailored to fit the product's needs, while the rest relies on proven, ready-made components. This approach offers flexibility and quicker design time. Full-Custom ASICs, on the other hand, are designed entirely from scratch. Every single part of the chip, right down to the smallest details, is created specifically for the task at hand. This allows for the highest performance, but it also takes more time and costs more to develop.

In terms of customization, semi-custom chips provide a balance between using standard components and customizing only the parts that need it. This is great for products that require some customization but don't need to reinvent the wheel. Full-custom chips, however, give total control over the design, allowing for complete customization of the entire chip. This makes full-custom ASICs perfect for applications where performance and power efficiency are critical.

## 7.3.3 Application-Specific Integrated Circuit (ASIC) Design Flow

The process of designing an Application-Specific Integrated Circuit (ASIC) follows a structured, step-by-step methodology known as the ASIC Design Flow. Each stage plays a crucial role in ensuring that the chip meets performance, functionality, and reliability standards. Below are the key steps involved in the design flow:

## Design Entry:

This is the starting point of the ASIC design process. At this stage, the design's microarchitecture is implemented using Hardware Description Languages (HDLs) such as VHDL, Verilog, or System Verilog. These languages describe the functionality and structure of the chip.

## Logic Synthesis:

In this step, the design written in HDL is converted into a netlist-a list of logic gates and their connections. The netlist includes the types of logic cells, interconnections, and other necessary components for the application. This synthesis ensures that the design is ready for the next stages.

<!-- page: 141 -->

<!-- review-required: visual-evidence -->

Visual evidence on page 141 requires source review; no verified description is available.

<!-- review-end -->

## System Partitioning:

The overall design is often too large to handle as a single block. During system partitioning, the large die is divided into smaller, manageable ASIC-sized pieces. This helps streamline the process and makes it easier to manage the complexity of the design.

## Pre-Layout Simulation:

Before moving further, the design undergoes a pre-layout simulation to check for functional correctness. This is essential to ensure that the system behaves as expected and any errors in logic or design are identified and fixed.

## Floor Planning:

The netlist is now used to determine the basic layout of the blocks on the chip. This involves arranging the functional blocks in an optimal manner, considering factors like size and connectivity.

## Placement:

Once the floor plan is established, the exact placement of cells within the blocks is determined. This step ensures that the components are positioned in a way that minimizes routing complexity and optimizes performance.

## Routing:

After placement, the next step is to create the actual connections (wires) between the blocks and cells. Routing ensures that the electrical signals flow properly between different parts of the design.

## Extraction:

In this step, the electrical properties of the interconnections, such as resistance and capacitance, are calculated. This helps in understanding how the physical layout might impact the chip's performance.

## Post-Layout Simulation:

Before final submission for manufacturing, a post-layout simulation is conducted. This ensures that the chip functions correctly under real-world conditions, including the added load of interconnects. If issues are found, they are corrected before moving forward.

## 7.5 ELECTROMIGRATION

Electromigration occurs when electrons moving through a conductor transfer momentum to metal atoms, causing these atoms to migrate and leading to material degradation over time. This phenomenon can impact the reliability and longevity of electronic components, making it crucial to address it during the design and manufacturing processes.

To prevent electromigration, several design strategies can be employed. One approach is to reduce current density by using wider interconnects, which ensures that power-carrying interconnects are robust enough to handle the current without excessive density. Additionally, incorporating multiple bond pads for power and ground connections helps distribute the current more evenly across the chip.

Optimizing the layout is also essential. By minimizing the length of interconnects that carry high currents, resistance and heat generation are reduced. Designing the layout to be symmetrical can further help distribute current evenly and minimize hotspots.

In terms of Power Distribution Network (PDN) design, creating a robust PDN minimizes voltage drops and ensures an even distribution of current across the chip. Adequate decoupling capacitors placed close to power pins smooth out current spikes and reduce stress on interconnects.

Material selection plays a significant role in managing electromigration. Using highresilience metals such as copper, which is preferred over aluminum for its better resistance to electromigration, is advantageous. Barrier layers like titanium nitride (TiN) or tantalum nitride (TaN) can be employed to prevent metal diffusion. Advanced metallization techniques, such as alloying copper with elements like manganese or aluminum and ensuring high-quality electroplated copper with fine-grain structures, further enhance resistance to electromigration.

Printed Circuit Boards (PCBs) represent a crucial technology that organizes and facilitates the connection of electronic components. Comprising conductive pathways, or traces, constructed from copper sheets that are laminated onto a non-conducting insulating substrate, PCBs enable the interconnection and support of electronic components on the board. These pathways are formed through a "printing" process, which negates the need for soldering and external electrical power.

PCBs are foundational to the operation of most modern electrical devices and find application across a range of domains. In consumer electronics, they are integral to smartphones, tablets, radios, and televisions, serving essential roles in communication devices, computers, and entertainment systems. In industrial applications, custom PCBs automate processes, thereby enhancing efficiency and reducing costs in machinery such as assembly lines, power equipment, and measurement devices. ## 7.8.5 Solder Mask Manufacturing Process:

The process begins with cleaning the board to ensure it is free from dust and moisture. It is essential to verify that the board is completely dry before proceeding. Once cleaned, the board is placed into a vertical coater for the application of the solder mask ink. The thickness of the solder mask is determined based on factors such as the number of electronic components, reliability requirements, and the thickness of the board.

Following the application, the board undergoes a pre-hardening stage, where the ink coating is solidified and any excess material is removed. This prepares the board for the next step, which involves imaging and hardening. During this phase, a transparent film containing circuit images is applied to the board, and the board is exposed to UV light. The areas of the board not covered by the circuit images will harden, while those covered by the images remain in their pre-hardened state. Careful alignment of the transparent film is critical to ensure proper placement of the copper foil, as misalignment can compromise the entire process.

Next, the board is placed into a developer to remove any excess solder mask, revealing the areas designated for copper foil implantation. After development, the board undergoes a final hardening to solidify the ink coating, followed by a thorough cleaning to eliminate any remaining dust particles. At this stage, the surface of the board can be smoothed, the copper foils integrated, and the electronic components assembled.

## 7.8.7 Using Gerber Files in PCB Fabrication PCB to Gerber:

In the realm of PCB fabrication, Gerber files serve as the essential link between design and manufacturing. The process begins with engineers and designers employing advanced PCB design software to craft a digital representation of the circuit. This digital model consists of several layers, each specifying distinct parameters such as copper traces, solder masks, and silkscreen elements. Upon completion of the design, the software generates corresponding Gerber files, with each file representing a specific layer of the PCB and containing the requisite layout and feature details crucial for fabrication.

Gerber files for copper layers precisely define the locations, widths, and configurations of traces, pads, and board components, ensuring accuracy during the copper etching process. Similarly, solder mask layers are outlined by corresponding Gerber files, which detail

where solder masks are to be applied, ensuring proper protection during the soldering of components. The silkscreen layers, conveyed through Gerber files, include labeling, component outlines, and part numbers, facilitating accurate component placement and identification. In addition, drill files, derived from Gerber data, provide the precise coordinates for all holes and vias on the board, directing the drilling machinery with high accuracy.

The transition to manufacturing begins with photo-plotting, where Gerber files are fed into photoplotters-machines that translate the ASCII-based Gerber data into high-resolution images of each PCB layer. These images serve as the foundation for creating films or masks, which guide various manufacturing processes such as copper etching and solder mask application. During etching, the Gerber files meticulously direct the chemical process to ensure that only the required copper traces remain, while undesired copper is removed. Likewise, drilling machines are precisely guided by the Gerber data to create holes and vias at exact locations. Solder masks are applied following the specifications in the Gerber files to protect the board during component assembly, and silkscreen printing is conducted based on the silkscreen files to accurately position labels and other critical details.

Throughout the manufacturing process, quality control measures are implemented to ensure that the fabricated boards conform to the original Gerber files. This comparison verifies the accuracy of each stage, ensuring that the final product meets the design specifications. The Gerber files also contain alignment marks and reference points, which facilitate the accurate overlay and registration of the multiple PCB layers, an essential aspect of ensuring proper layer stacking and functionality.

The use of Gerber files adheres to an industry-standard format, ensuring consistency and compatibility across different manufacturing platforms and processes. This standardized approach streamlines communication between designers and manufacturers, reducing the risk of errors and misinterpretations during the transition from design to production. Furthermore, modern Gerber file formats, such as Gerber X2, offer enhanced support for advanced design features, including material properties, impedance profiles, and complex aperture definitions, providing manufacturers with the necessary detail to produce highly specialized and advanced PCB designs.

It is important to note that while the Gerber file format itself does not have a predefined file extension, various extensions are used to distinguish the purpose and content of each file, such as .gbr for general Gerber files or .drill for drill data, ensuring clarity and organization throughout the fabrication workflow.
